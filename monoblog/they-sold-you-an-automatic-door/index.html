They Sold You an Automatic Door.

The doorman fallacy, starring Meta, Google and the marketer who forgot why the hotel exists

By Gaurav Mishra · 28 October 2026 · 7 min read
At a glance
  1. Meta and Google have automated the visible job of marketing: finding the next click and crowning the next "winning" creative. They do it brilliantly.
  2. Then they sold that job as the whole job. Too many marketers bought it, and shrank their own role to match.
  3. A company needs one of each: a performance marketer to run the door, and a brand marketer to stand at it.

The hotel that saved money.

Rory Sutherland tells a story about a hotel. A consultant looks at the doorman and writes down his job: opens the door. An automatic door does that for a fraction of the cost. So the hotel installs one and lets the doorman go.

The door works perfectly. It opens every single time.

Then the hotel slowly gets worse. Nobody recognises the regulars or keeps the wrong people out of the lobby anymore. Nobody hails a taxi in the rain, and the place stops feeling like somewhere worth paying extra for. None of that was written in the job description, so none of it showed up in the savings calculation. It showed up later, in the room rates.

Sutherland called it the doorman fallacy: you define a job by its most visible task, automate that task, and lose everything the job quietly did.

Hold that hotel in your head. Now open your ads manager.

The automatic door, now with AI.

Harvests more than it plants
Less autonomous than "AI" suggests

Meta calls it Advantage+. Google calls it Performance Max. The pitch is roughly the same: give us your budget, your creatives and a goal, and our AI will find your customers. Fewer settings, better results.

And for the visible job, they are better than any human alive. They can test a thousand combinations before your team finishes its morning chai. They find the person most likely to click and the person most likely to buy this week. I have run these machines. They are extraordinary at opening the door.

Here is the part the pitch skips.

They grade their own homework. The platform that spent your money is the same platform that tells you how well it was spent. Every conversion it can claim, it claims. (Including, conveniently, the ones that would have happened anyway.)

They harvest more than they plant. An algorithm told to find buyers at the lowest cost will go looking for people who were already on their way in. That is efficient. It also means the machine gets very good at taking credit for demand it never created, and has zero incentive to create the next wave.

They are far less autonomous than the word "AI" suggests. Zoom out and look at the bird's-eye view. A human chose the objective. A human set the budget. A human made the creatives the machine is choosing between. A human picked the metric it is judged by. The machine optimises the proxy it was handed, with stunning precision, and has no opinion on whether the proxy was the right one. Hand it the wrong target, and it will hit the wrong target faster than any team ever could.

That is the automatic door. It opens beautifully. It has no idea who it is letting in, or what kind of hotel it belongs to.

The marketer who became a door.

The platforms committed the fallacy on purpose. It is good business for them.

The tragedy is the marketers who committed it on themselves.

Somewhere along the way, a lot of growth and performance roles shrank to one question: which creative is winning? The dashboard turns green, the winning ad gets more budget, the losing ad gets killed, and everyone goes home feeling data-driven.

But "winning" only means winning on the number the machine was told to chase. And the creative that wins on cost per purchase is very often the discount, the countdown timer, the fake urgency, the before-and-after that borders on a lie. It wins this week. It also teaches every customer who sees it what your brand is: a thing you buy only when it is cheap.

Nobody fired the doorman. They turned him into a door.

A hypothetical brand, painfully familiar.

Picture a D2C skincare brand. Good product, honest founders, real ingredients. Performance is the growth engine, run with discipline, and the dashboard looks great.

Month one, the 30%-off creative beats the ingredient story on cost per purchase. Budget moves. Month three, a sharper discount beats the 30%. Budget moves again. Month six, the account is a wall of offers, and the cost per purchase looks better than ever.

Now look at what the dashboard was never built to show. Fewer people search for the brand by name. Full-price orders are a shrinking share of the total. Customers who arrived on a discount wait for the next one before they buy again. The founder's favourite product, the one that made the brand worth starting, barely appears in any ad, because it never won a test.

Every weekly report said the machine was working. It was. It was just working on the wrong hotel.

(Before anyone writes in: yes, the brand is invented. If it sounds like your brand, that is the point.)

Excuses, or a case for existence.

What it should mean
Demand worth harvesting
Green for whom?

Here is where the performance crowd rolls its eyes. Brand marketers, they say, love anything that cannot be measured, because then nobody can measure them.

Fair. Some brand marketers do hide behind fog. That is a bad brand marketer, and the answer to a bad doorman was never an automatic door.

A good brand marketer does the jobs the dashboard cannot see:

  • Deciding who the brand is for, and just as clearly, who it is not for, so the machine is told whom to chase.
  • Deciding what the brand should mean in a customer's head a year from now, and refusing creative that wins this week by eroding that.
  • Creating the demand that performance will later harvest, through stories, community, PR, content and product.
  • Asking the uncomfortable question when every number is green: green for whom, and for how long?

None of that shows up in a ROAS column. All of it shows up in the room rate.

One of each.

So let me be clear about which side I am on: both. I want the automatic door. I want it fast, cheap and tested to death.

I also want a doorman.

Every company needs one of each. The performance marketer runs the door: the platforms, the bids, the tests, the cost of every single walk-in. The brand marketer stands at it: who comes in, what they feel when they do, and whether they come back without a coupon.

Keep only the door, and you get a cheaper hotel that slowly empties. Keep only the doorman, and you get a charming lobby nobody can find. The companies that grow for a decade, the ones that compound, have both, and make them argue every week.

Meta and Google will keep selling you the door. They are very good at it. Just remember who wrote the job description.

What I'd do on Monday.

  1. 1
    Put a brand brief above every campaign. One page: who this is for, what it must never say, what we want them to remember. The machine optimises inside it, never around it.
  2. 2
    Judge creative on two clocks. This week's cost per purchase, and next quarter's brand search, repeat rate and full-price share. A creative that wins one clock and loses the other does not get scaled.
  3. 3
    Measure what the platform cannot grade. Run holdout tests, track branded search and repeat purchase outside the ads manager, and stop letting the seller audit the sale.
  4. 4
    Give the doorman a veto. The brand marketer can stop any creative that wins by lying about the brand, however good its numbers look.
